# Project Overview  <a name="overview-main"></a>

### Context <a name="overview-context"></a>

A fast-food chain was planning to add a new item to its menu, and was undecided between three possible promotions to launch it.

To find out which promotion works best, the new item was introduced at 137 randomly selected locations, with each location receiving one of the three promotions. Weekly sales of the new item were then recorded at each location for the first four weeks.

The client wants to understand:

* Whether average weekly sales per store are meeting their target of $50k
* Whether there is a significant difference in sales between the promotions
* Whether sales changed over the course of the campaign

This will allow them to choose the promotion with the best return, and make more informed decisions about future campaigns!

<br>
<br>
### Actions <a name="overview-actions"></a>

As each of these questions compares *average sales* (a numeric value), rather than *rates* as in our Chi-Square project, we applied three types of T-Test - one for each question:

| **Business Question** | **Test** | **Why** |
|---|---|---|
| Are average weekly sales different to the $50k target? | One Sample T-Test | One group, compared to a known value |
| Do Promotion 1 stores sell more than Promotion 2 stores? | Independent Samples T-Test | Two groups made up of *different* stores |
| Did sales change between week 1 and week 4? | Paired T-Test | The *same* stores, measured twice |

Before running any tests, we noticed that our dataset has 548 rows, but only 137 stores - each store appears four times (once per week). As the T-Test assumes that each observation is independent, and a store's four weekly sales are closely related to each other, we collapsed the data down to *one row per store* (each store's average weekly sales) for the One Sample and Independent Samples tests.

For each test we set out our hypotheses, and used a Significance Level of 0.05.

<br>
<br>

### Results & Discussion <a name="overview-results"></a>

**One Sample T-Test**

* Average weekly sales per store: **$53.47k**
* t-statistic: **2.51**, p-value: **0.013**

We reject the null hypothesis - on average, stores sold significantly *more* than the $50k target.

**Independent Samples T-Test**

* Promotion 1: **$58.1k** average weekly sales per store
* Promotion 2: **$47.3k** average weekly sales per store
* t-statistic: **3.33**, p-value: **0.0013**

We reject the null hypothesis - Promotion 1 stores sold around **$10.8k more per store, per week** than Promotion 2 stores.

**Paired T-Test**

* t-statistic: **-0.98**, p-value: **0.33**

We retain the null hypothesis, as there is not enough evidence that sales changed between week 1 and week 4 of the campaign.

Based on these results, we would recommend Promotion 1 over Promotion 2. As we did not compare Promotion 1 with Promotion 3 in this project, running that comparison would be a good next step before making a final decision!

# Concept Overview  <a name="concept-overview"></a>

<br>
#### Hypothesis Testing

A Hypothesis Test is used to assess the plausibility of an assumed viewpoint based on sample data - in other words, it helps us assess whether the data we've collected gives us enough evidence to move away from that viewpoint.

<br>
**The Null Hypothesis**

In any Hypothesis Test, we start with the Null Hypothesis. This is our initial viewpoint, and it is always that there is no difference, no effect, or no relationship.

<br>
**The Alternate Hypothesis**

The Alternate Hypothesis is the opposite viewpoint - that there *is* a difference, an effect, or a relationship. If we reject the Null Hypothesis, we are supporting the Alternate Hypothesis.

<br>
**The Significance Level**

Before we run any numbers, we specify a Significance Level. This is the p-value threshold at which we'll decide to reject or retain the Null Hypothesis. It is essentially a line we draw in the sand saying "if there really was no difference, how often am I willing to wrongly conclude that there is one?" A Significance Level of 0.05 means we accept a 5% chance of this kind of false positive.

<br>
**The p-value**

The p-value tells us how likely we would be to see a result at least as extreme as ours, *if the Null Hypothesis were true*. A small p-value means our result would be surprising if there was really no difference - which gives us evidence against the Null Hypothesis.

<br>
#### The T-Test

A T-Test compares averages. Every T-Test calculates a t-statistic, which follows the same basic idea:

**t = (what we observed - what the Null Hypothesis expects) / standard error**

The standard error measures how much noise we would expect in our average, based on how spread out the data is and how many observations we have. The bigger the t-statistic (positive or negative), the further our result is from what the Null Hypothesis expects, and the smaller the p-value.

The sign of the t-statistic tells us the *direction* - for example, a positive t-statistic in a One Sample T-Test means our average is *above* the value we are testing against.

<br>
**One Sample T-Test** - compares the average of one group to a known or target value.

**Independent Samples T-Test** - compares the averages of two separate, unrelated groups. We use *Welch's* version of this test, which does not assume that both groups have the same spread of data.

**Paired T-Test** - compares two measurements taken on the *same* subjects (for example, before & after). It works by calculating the difference for each subject, and then testing whether the average difference is zero - in other words, it is a One Sample T-Test on the differences.

# Data Overview & Preparation  <a name="data-overview"></a>

The data for this project comes from the [Fast Food Marketing Campaign A/B Test](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/chebotinaa/fast-food-marketing-campaign-ab-test) dataset on Kaggle.

In the code below, we:

* Load in the Python libraries we require for importing the data and performing the T-Tests (using scipy)
* Import the data
* Collapse the data down to one row per store

<br>
```python

# import the required python libraries
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

# import data
sales_data = pd.read_csv("data/WA_Marketing-Campaign.csv")

# collapse to one row per store (each store's average weekly sales)
store_data = sales_data.groupby(["LocationID", "Promotion"])["SalesInThousands"].mean().reset_index()

```
<br>
In the raw data we have:

* MarketID
* MarketSize (Small, Medium or Large)
* LocationID
* AgeOfStore
* Promotion (1, 2 or 3)
* week (1 to 4)
* SalesInThousands

The data has 548 rows and no missing values. Each of the 137 stores received just *one* promotion, and had its sales recorded for four weeks - giving us 137 x 4 = 548 rows.

<br>
#### Why We Collapse To One Row Per Store

If we were to feed all 548 rows straight into a T-Test, we would be treating each store's four weeks as four independent pieces of evidence - when really they all come from the same store, with the same customers and the same staff.

As the standard error is calculated as *standard deviation / √n*, using n = 548 instead of n = 137 would make our standard error roughly half the size it should be. This would inflate our t-statistic, shrink our p-value, and make us more likely to find "significant" results that aren't really there. 

By using each store's average weekly sales, we have 137 truly independent observations.

<br>
# One Sample T-Test <a name="one-sample"></a>

**Business question:** The client set a target of $50k average weekly sales per store. Are stores meeting it?

<br>
#### State Hypotheses & Significance Level

```python

# specify hypotheses & significance level for test
null_hypothesis = "The average weekly sales per store is equal to the $50k target"
alternate_hypothesis = "The average weekly sales per store is not equal to the $50k target"
alpha = 0.05
target = 50

```

<br>
#### Run The Test

```python

# sample summary
print(f"Mean: {store_data['SalesInThousands'].mean():.2f}")
print(f"Std:  {store_data['SalesInThousands'].std():.2f}")
print(f"n:    {len(store_data)}")

# run the one sample t-test
t_statistic, p_value = stats.ttest_1samp(store_data["SalesInThousands"], popmean=target)

print(f"t-statistic: {t_statistic:.4f}")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.4f}")

>> Mean: 53.47
>> Std:  16.19
>> n:    137
>> t-statistic: 2.5052
>> p-value: 0.0134

```

<br>
#### Analysing The Results

```python

# print the results
if p_value <= alpha:
    print(f"As our p-value of {p_value:.4f} is lower than our significance level of {alpha} - we reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that: {alternate_hypothesis}")
else:
    print(f"As our p-value of {p_value:.4f} is higher than our significance level of {alpha} - we retain the null hypothesis, as there is not enough evidence that average sales differ from the target")

>> As our p-value of 0.0134 is lower than our significance level of 0.05 - we reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that: The average weekly sales per store is not equal to the $50k target

```

<br>
As our p-value of **0.013** is lower than our Significance Level of 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis. As our t-statistic is *positive* (and our average of $53.47k is above $50k), we can say that stores sold significantly *more* than the target.

The 95% confidence interval for average weekly sales per store is around **$50.7k to $56.2k**. As the whole interval sits above $50k, this tells the same story as our p-value - but also gives the client a sense of how far above target stores are likely to be.

# Independent Samples T-Test <a name="independent-samples"></a>

**Business question:** Promotion 1 had the highest average weekly sales at $58.1k. Do stores with Promotion 1 really sell more than stores with Promotion 2?

As each store only received one promotion, these are two groups of *different* stores - so we use the Independent Samples T-Test. We exclude Promotion 3 stores, so that we are comparing just two groups.

<br>
#### State Hypotheses & Significance Level

```python

# specify hypotheses & significance level for test
null_hypothesis = "There is no difference in average weekly sales between Promotion 1 and Promotion 2"
alternate_hypothesis = "There is a difference in average weekly sales between Promotion 1 and Promotion 2"
alpha = 0.05

```

<br>
#### Run The Test

```python

# split stores into the two groups we're comparing
promo1 = store_data.loc[store_data["Promotion"] == 1, "SalesInThousands"]
promo2 = store_data.loc[store_data["Promotion"] == 2, "SalesInThousands"]

# summary of each group
print(f"Promotion 1 - n: {len(promo1)}, mean: {promo1.mean():.2f}, std: {promo1.std():.2f}")
print(f"Promotion 2 - n: {len(promo2)}, mean: {promo2.mean():.2f}, std: {promo2.std():.2f}")

# run the independent samples t-test (Welch's - does not assume equal variances)
t_statistic, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(promo1, promo2, equal_var=False)

print(f"t-statistic: {t_statistic:.4f}")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.4f}")

# difference in average weekly sales
mean_difference = promo1.mean() - promo2.mean()
print(f"Difference in average weekly sales (Promotion 1 - Promotion 2): ${mean_difference:.2f}k")

>> Promotion 1 - n: 43, mean: 58.10, std: 16.03
>> Promotion 2 - n: 47, mean: 47.33, std: 14.50
>> t-statistic: 3.3321
>> p-value: 0.0013
>> Difference in average weekly sales (Promotion 1 - Promotion 2): $10.77k

```

<br>
#### Analysing The Results

```python

# print the results
if p_value <= alpha:
    print(f"As our p-value of {p_value:.4f} is lower than our significance level of {alpha} - we reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that: {alternate_hypothesis}")
    print(f"Promotion 1 outperformed Promotion 2 by ${mean_difference:.2f}k per store per week")
else:
    print(f"As our p-value of {p_value:.4f} is higher than our significance level of {alpha} - we retain the null hypothesis, as there is not enough evidence of a difference between the two promotions")

>> As our p-value of 0.0013 is lower than our significance level of 0.05 - we reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that: There is a difference in average weekly sales between Promotion 1 and Promotion 2
>> Promotion 1 outperformed Promotion 2 by $10.77k per store per week

```

<br>
With a t-statistic of **3.33**, the difference between the two promotions is over 3 standard errors wide - well beyond what we would expect from random variation alone. As our p-value of **0.0013** is lower than our Significance Level of 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that Promotion 1 stores sold around **$10.8k more per store, per week** than Promotion 2 stores.

Interestingly, the difference here (around $10.8k) is roughly three times larger than the difference in our One Sample T-Test (around $3.5k), but the t-statistic only rose from 2.51 to 3.33. This is because each group here only has 43 or 47 stores (rather than 137), and comparing two groups means the noise from *both* groups feeds into the standard error.

# Paired T-Test <a name="paired"></a>

**Business question:** Did store sales change between week 1 and week 4 of the campaign - for example, did the promotions build momentum, or fade?

As we are comparing the *same* 137 stores at two points in time, we use the Paired T-Test. For this test we go back to our weekly data, rather than the store averages.

<br>
#### State Hypotheses & Significance Level

```python

# specify hypotheses & significance level for test
null_hypothesis = "There is no difference in average store sales between week 1 and week 4"
alternate_hypothesis = "There is a difference in average store sales between week 1 and week 4"
alpha = 0.05

```

<br>
#### Run The Test

```python

# reshape: one row per store, one column per week
weekly_sales = sales_data.pivot(index="LocationID", columns="week", values="SalesInThousands")

week1 = weekly_sales[1]
week4 = weekly_sales[4]
differences = week4 - week1

# run the paired t-test
t_statistic, p_value = stats.ttest_rel(week4, week1)

print(f"t-statistic: {t_statistic:.4f}")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.4f}")

>> t-statistic: -0.9751
>> p-value: 0.3312

```

<br>
#### Analysing The Results

```python

# print the results
if p_value <= alpha:
    print(f"As our p-value of {p_value:.4f} is lower than our significance level of {alpha} - we reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that: {alternate_hypothesis}")
else:
    print(f"As our p-value of {p_value:.4f} is higher than our significance level of {alpha} - we retain the null hypothesis, as there is not enough evidence that sales changed between week 1 and week 4")

>> As our p-value of 0.3312 is higher than our significance level of 0.05 - we retain the null hypothesis, as there is not enough evidence that sales changed between week 1 and week 4

```

<br>
Our t-statistic is negative, meaning week 4 sales were slightly *lower* than week 1 on average - but with a p-value of **0.33**, this difference is well within what we would expect from random variation. We do not have evidence that the promotions built momentum or faded over the four weeks.

<br>
#### Why Pairing Matters

To understand the Paired T-Test better, we ran two extra checks:

```python

# check 1: a paired t-test is a one sample t-test on the differences
print(stats.ttest_1samp(differences, popmean=0))

>> TtestResult(statistic=-0.9751, pvalue=0.3312, df=136)

# check 2: what if we wrongly ignored the pairing?
print(stats.ttest_ind(week4, week1, equal_var=False))

>> TtestResult(statistic=-0.2810, pvalue=0.7789, df=271.99)

```

Check 1 gives exactly the same result as our Paired T-Test - confirming that a Paired T-Test is simply a One Sample T-Test on the differences, testing whether the average difference is zero.

Check 2 shows what happens if we ignore the pairing. The t-statistic shrinks to less than a third of its size, and the p-value jumps from 0.33 to 0.78.

This is because stores vary a lot from each other - some sell around $30k a week, others far more. But a big store is big in *both* week 1 and week 4. When we take the difference for each store, this store-to-store variation cancels out, leaving only how much each store *changed*. The Independent Samples T-Test can't do this, so all of that store-to-store noise ends up in the standard error, drowning out the signal.

In this case both versions are non-significant, so our conclusion doesn't change - but with a real effect, the Paired T-Test could detect it where the Independent Samples T-Test would miss it.

# Discussion <a name="discussion"></a>

Across our three tests, we found that:

| **Test** | **Business Question** | **Result** | **Conclusion** |
|---|---|---|---|
| One Sample T-Test | Are stores meeting the $50k target? | t = 2.51, p = 0.013 | Stores averaged significantly *above* target (around $53.5k) |
| Independent Samples T-Test | Promotion 1 vs Promotion 2? | t = 3.33, p = 0.0013 | Promotion 1 sold around $10.8k more per store, per week |
| Paired T-Test | Did sales change from week 1 to week 4? | t = -0.98, p = 0.33 | Not enough evidence that sales changed |

Based on these results, Promotion 1 clearly outperformed Promotion 2 - and choosing Promotion 2 could cost the client around $10.8k per store, per week.

However, we only compared Promotion 1 with Promotion 2. Running the same test for Promotion 1 vs Promotion 3 would tell us whether Promotion 1 is the clear winner, or simply better than Promotion 2. As we would then be running multiple tests, a method such as ANOVA, or adjusting our Significance Level for multiple comparisons, would also be worth exploring!

Two key lessons came out of this project:

* **Aggregating to the right level matters.** Our data had 548 rows, but only 137 independent stores - using all rows would have made our results look more significant than they really were.
* **Matching the test to the design matters.** Different stores call for an Independent Samples T-Test, while the same stores measured twice call for a Paired T-Test - and using the wrong one can hide real effects.
